# CBAM Verification Reports for Asian Steel: A Growing Risk for EU Importers

European buyers are receiving emissions verification reports from Asian steel mills that contain data inconsistencies. Under CBAM, relying on flawed documentation could cost importers far more than the steel itself.

## The Problem: Verification Reports That Do Not Add Up

Since January 1, 2026, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) requires EU importers of in-scope steel goods to declare verified embedded emissions for every tonne imported. The alternative is paying CBAM duties on **default values** — which, for some products and origins, can exceed the base price of the steel itself.

Industry reports indicate that a significant number of verification documents being circulated by Asian steel exporters contain data that would not withstand scrutiny by independent reviewers or EU national authorities. These reports are being issued not only by smaller consultants but also by internationally recognized certification bodies — making the risk harder for buyers to detect.

Specific issues observed in verification reports include:

**✓** **Emissions figures below physically achievable thresholds** for integrated blast furnace-basic oxygen furnace (BF-BOF) production — a route that industry benchmarks place at approximately 1.8–2.2 tCO₂/tonne for direct emissions alone

**✓** **Omission of precursor data** — iron ore, ferroalloys, and other upstream inputs excluded from calculations

**✓** **Mass-balancing errors** — reported production volumes not aligning with installation capacity

**✓** **Misclassification of emissions** — direct emissions presented as indirect emissions, making total footprints appear compliant while direct intensity remains hidden

**✓** **Assessment limited to export quantities** rather than total installation output, artificially lowering weighted average values

When these reports are submitted to EU CBAM authorities as part of an importer's declaration, the risk is clear: **rejection of "actual" emissions data, fallback to default values, and punitive costs that erase any base price advantage.**

## Why This Matters: The September 2027 Deadline

Following the March 31, 2026 deadline to register as an authorized CBAM declarant, importers now face their first formal reporting obligation: **CBAM declarations for embedded emissions imported in calendar year 2026 are due in September 2027.**

Here is what that means in practice:

**Scenario A: Verified "Actual" Emissions Accepted**

Importer submits verified emissions data from a compliant mill.  
CBAM cost: **Minimal to zero** (depending on free allocation and benchmark comparisons).

**Scenario B: Verification Rejected — Fallback to Defaults**

Importer submits data that EU authorities deem insufficient or inaccurate.  
CBAM cost: **Up to €200/tonne or more** — depending on product, origin, and default value methodology.

The gap between these two scenarios is not marginal. For a 300-tonne order of hot-rolled coil, the difference between accepted verification and default values can run into **tens of thousands of euros** — effectively nullifying any savings from choosing a lower base-price supplier.

And here is the critical point: **by the time an importer discovers their verification is rejected, there may be insufficient time to remedy the data, engage a new verifier, or source from an alternative mill before the declaration deadline.** The steel has already landed. The liability is fixed.

## The Risks: What Importers Face Today

### 1\. The "Pre-Verification" Trap

Many verification reports currently circulating are based on 2025 data or preliminary assessments — not the calendar year 2026 data that CBAM declarations will require. These "pre-verifications" give buyers a false sense of security. They are not binding. They are not guaranteed to align with the final 2026 installation data. And they will need to be re-assessed before September 2027.

Importers who treat pre-verification reports as final documentation are building their sourcing strategy on unstable ground.

### 2\. The Competitive Pressure to Ignore Red Flags

Market dynamics are making the problem worse. Importers report that competitors are circulating verification reports showing minimal or zero CBAM liability for integrated BF-BOF steel — figures that industry experts describe as physically impossible. The superficial attractiveness of these low numbers creates pressure across the supply chain: _"If their supplier can deliver at near-zero CBAM cost, why can't ours?"_

The answer, in many cases, is that those numbers will not survive independent review. But by the time that becomes clear, contracts are signed, shipments are en route, and the importer is locked into a liability they did not price for.

### 3\. The Consolidation Squeeze

As awareness of verification risks grows, buyers are already consolidating sourcing toward a smaller group of larger mills considered more likely to pass scrutiny. These mills command a price premium — not because their base steel is more expensive, but because their documentation is credible.

Importers who delay this consolidation risk being left with two bad options: pay the premium for verified supply, or gamble on unverified supply and face default values.

### 4\. The Intermediary Risk

The verification problem is not limited to direct EU imports. Steel shipped to intermediary hubs — Turkey, for example — and then re-exported to Europe still requires verified emission data at the point of EU entry. If the original mill's verification is flawed, the re-exporter's documentation collapses with it.

While precise figures are difficult to verify, industry estimates suggest that a substantial majority of Asian steel exporters are not yet prepared to produce defensible CBAM verification reports:

**Chinese Exporters**

Estimated readiness for EU-recognized verification: **10–15% of tier-1 mills**. The majority of producers still rely on self-declared data or have not completed third-party audits aligned with CBAM's unique requirements.

**Indian Exporters**

Estimated readiness: **~25% of integrated and specialist mills**. The verified subset includes some of the lowest-carbon producers globally (scrap-based EAF routes as low as 0.684 tCO₂/tonne), but the fragmented mini-mill sector lags significantly.

**Japanese Exporters**

Estimated readiness: **~80%**. Premium pricing, but the lowest compliance risk and strongest documentation standards.

**Vietnamese Exporters**

Estimated readiness: **~10%**. Emerging capabilities, but high default risk for now.

The pattern is consistent across regions: **verification readiness, not geography, determines total cost under CBAM.**

## What to Ask Your Supplier — Regardless of Origin

Before placing any order with an Asian mill, confirm these four items. If your supplier cannot provide all four, you are exposed to default value risk:

**1.** **Verified emissions certificate reference.** Who conducted the verification? (TUV, SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, DNV). Is the verification current for 2026? Is the verifier accredited under EU-recognized standards?

**2.** **Installation-level data.** Is the verification specific to the furnace and rolling mill that will produce your steel? Corporate averages are not sufficient — CBAM requires installation-level reporting.

**3.** **CN code coverage.** Does the certificate cover your specific product category? A mill verified for hot-rolled coil (CN 7208) may not be verified for bars (CN 7214) or wire rod (CN 7213).

**4.** **CBAM Communication Template (CT) v2.1.1.** Request the actual file submitted to the EU registry. The assurance statement alone is not enough — you need the structured data file that authorities will cross-reference.

If any of these four are missing, incomplete, or evasive, the risk is not theoretical. It is a **€200/tonne default penalty** waiting to be applied.
