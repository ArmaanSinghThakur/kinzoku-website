# CBAM Default Values for Indian Steel: The Hidden Cost

Most Indian mills cannot provide verified emissions data. If you import bars, wire rods, wire, bright bars, coils, or tubes from India, the EU will charge you the maximum carbon rate. Here is what that costs — and how to avoid it.

## Why Indian Steel Is Under CBAM Scrutiny

**India is the second-largest steel exporter to the EU** after China. For European manufacturers, Indian steel offers competitive pricing, a wide range of grades, and established mill relationships. But since January 1, 2026, a new factor has changed the cost equation entirely: **CBAM default values.**

The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism requires importers to declare the exact amount of CO₂ embedded in every tonne of steel they bring into Europe. If the mill cannot provide verified, third-party emissions data, the EU applies a **default value** — a worst-case assumption based on the most carbon-intensive production methods.

For Indian steel, this is a serious problem. A significant share of Indian production comes from **induction furnace and smaller blast furnace operations** that lack the granular tracking systems needed to isolate and report embedded emissions according to EU-validated system boundaries.

The result? **Most Indian mills default to the maximum CBAM rate.** And that maximum rate is far more expensive than buyers realize — regardless of whether you are importing bars, wire rods, coils, or tubes.

## What Default Values Cost by Product Category

The EU default value for direct emissions from steel production is approximately **2.5 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of steel**. This is based on the most carbon-intensive blast furnace operations, not the actual emissions of your specific mill or product.

## Why Bars and Wire Products Are Especially Vulnerable

Bars, wire rods, wire, and bright bars are Kinzoku's core focus — and they are also the categories where Indian default value exposure is **highest** for three reasons:

**1.** **Fragmented mill base.** Unlike HRC, which is dominated by large integrated mills (Tata, JSW, SAIL), the Indian bar and wire market includes dozens of smaller induction furnace operators. Most lack verification capability.

**2.** **Multiple processing stages.** A wire rod goes through rolling, pickling, drawing, and heat treatment. Each stage adds emissions. Without installation-level tracking, the EU applies the full default across every stage.

**3.** **Lower margins, higher impact.** Bars and wire products typically have thinner margins than flat products. A €56/tonne CBAM penalty can erase the entire cost advantage of sourcing from India.

For European fastener manufacturers, wire drawers, and precision engineering shops, this is existential. **Your €800/tonne Indian bright bar becomes €856/tonne overnight** — and your German competitor buying verified domestic material at €820/tonne now undercuts you.

## Why Most Indian Mills Cannot Verify

The problem is not that Indian mills are unwilling. It is that the verification infrastructure is not yet widespread. To provide EU-accepted emissions data, a mill must:

**1.** Install continuous emissions monitoring systems (CEMS) or use approved calculation methodologies

**2.** Document fuel mix, electricity source, and process emissions at the furnace level

**3.** Engage an EU-recognized third-party verifier (SGS, TUV, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, DNV) to audit and certify the data

**4.** Format the data according to EU Regulation 2023/956 and submit it through the CBAM Transitional Registry

For large integrated mills producing flat products, this is achievable. But for **smaller bar and wire mills** — which supply the majority of India's long product exports — these steps are out of reach.

The EU knows this. That is why the default value system exists. But for European buyers of bars and wire, the default value is a **financial penalty for sourcing from mills that have not invested in verification**.

## What to Ask Your Indian Supplier

Before placing any order with an Indian mill — for bars, wire, coils, or tubes — confirm these four items:

### 1\. Do You Have Verified Emissions Data for This Specific Product?

Request the verification certificate number, the verifying body (SGS, TUV, BV, Intertek, DNV), and the date of verification. A mill may be verified for HRC but not for wire rod. Product-specific verification matters.

### 2\. Is the Data at the Installation Level?

EU Regulation 2023/956 requires furnace-level or installation-level data, not corporate averages. A mill-wide average that includes low-carbon flat products and high-carbon long products is not acceptable for your bar or wire shipment.

### 3\. Can You Provide EN 10204 3.1 or 3.2 MTCs?

The material test certificate must link heat numbers to chemical analysis and continuous casting records. For wire and bright bars, this includes intermediate processing stages. This is separate from CBAM but equally critical for customs clearance.

### 4\. What Is Your Plan if Verification Fails?

If the mill's data is rejected by the EU verifier, what is the fallback? Some mills offer to cover the default value difference. Most do not. Get this in writing before you commit to volume.

If your supplier cannot answer these questions satisfactorily, you are buying into a **€48–64 per tonne penalty** with no recourse — regardless of product category.
